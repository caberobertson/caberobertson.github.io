## Problem and context

Dry EEG electrodes are what make a brain-computer interface usable outside a
lab: no gel, no long prep, no cleanup. They are also the part that most limits
signal quality, and the good ones are expensive enough to keep BCI work out of
reach for students and early-stage researchers. NeuroPawn Biotechnologies
builds affordable EEG hardware for exactly that audience, so the cost of the
electrode sits directly on top of their mission.

## Getting the project to exist

The project did not come down from a faculty list. I approached NeuroPawn,
proposed the work, and wrote the proposal that got it approved as a senior
capstone, along with the IRB protocol that governs testing on human subjects.

I lead a five-person team spanning four disciplines: electrical engineering,
engineering technology, mechanical engineering, and mechanical engineering
technology. That spread is deliberate. The electrode is simultaneously a
materials problem, a manufacturing problem, and an instrumentation problem, and
no single discipline covers it.

## Scope and method

The new electrode is measured against NeuroPawn's incumbent commercial
electrode on seven parameters: **contact impedance, contact area, DC offset,
noise, frequency response, shielding, and durability**. Materials work centers
on human-safe conductive silicones and plastic-embedded silicone methods, with
SLA and FDM printing for the substrate.

Bench numbers alone would not settle whether the electrode is actually usable,
so the team is also building a proof-of-concept BCI that steers a
remote-control car through a maze, and running it on both the new and the
incumbent electrodes. The comparison is the point: the same task, the same
pipeline, one variable changed.

## Where it stands

In progress. The team is assembled and the benchmark plan is set, but no
measurements are published here yet, and the electrode design is not frozen.
Results go on this page when there are results, not before.
